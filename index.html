Can We Trust the SOC Pilot? Evaluating Autonomous AI in Cybersecurity

October 14, 2026 · Critical Reflection · Agentic AI

AI can help security teams work faster, but responsible automation still depends on reliable information, clear limits, and people who know when to take control.

From Alert Handler to SOC Pilot

Imagine arriving at work and finding hundreds of cybersecurity alerts waiting to be investigated. Some may point to a real attack, while others may be harmless activity or false alarms. A security analyst must sort through the noise, gather evidence, and decide which situations need urgent attention. When alerts keep arriving throughout the day, it becomes difficult to investigate every event carefully.

This is the challenge discussed in John Velisaris's (2025) IBM article about building an autonomous security operations center, or SOC. Velisaris compares the future of security operations to a modern airliner: machines manage routine operations, while human analysts become "SOC pilots" who supervise the process and take control when something unfamiliar happens, such as a possible zero-day attack.

I find this model promising because it changes the analyst's role instead of simply removing it. Rather than repeating the same investigative steps, analysts could focus on unusual threats, complicated incidents, and decisions where a mistake could harm the organization. However, I do not think a SOC should operate with minimal human involvement just because an AI agent can complete tasks quickly. A capable agent can still misunderstand evidence, act on outdated information, or take an action that is technically reasonable but damaging to the business.

My view is that the SOC pilot model is realistic only when autonomy is introduced gradually and matched to the level of risk. AI should be allowed to handle routine work when its actions are tested and limited. People must retain meaningful control over high-impact decisions, and organizations must take responsibility for the systems and information they give their agents.

Is the Airline Comparison Realistic?

The airline comparison is useful because it highlights the difference between operating a system and supervising it. Modern aviation relies on automation, but pilots still need training, situational awareness, procedures, and the authority to intervene. The lesson is not that humans are unnecessary; it is that automation can handle predictable tasks while trained people manage exceptions and remain accountable for safe operation.

However, cybersecurity is not exactly like flying an airplane. Flight systems operate within defined procedures and physical limits, while attackers actively change their methods to avoid detection. Security information can be incomplete, the same action can have different consequences in different organizations, and an attack may deliberately confuse the tools used to detect it. A response that worked yesterday may not be safe in a new situation.

It is also important to distinguish a convincing vision from proof that fully autonomous SOCs are safe in every setting. Velisaris (2025) makes a case for greater autonomy and for giving agents the context needed to investigate and respond. That is a useful direction, but each organization still needs evidence that its own tools work under real conditions. Success should be judged not only by how quickly alerts are closed, but also by whether decisions are accurate, service disruptions are avoided, and incidents are handled properly.

For these reasons, I see the SOC pilot model as a realistic goal for specific tasks, not a reason to remove human analysts from the process entirely. Its responsible use depends on the type of incident, the reliability of the available context, the potential harm of the action, and the organization's ability to detect and correct mistakes.

Where AI Can Act Independently

AI is most suitable for work that is repetitive, well defined, and low risk. An agent can collect security logs, group duplicate alerts, summarize suspicious activity, compare events with known indicators of compromise, and retrieve details from endpoint or authentication tools. It can also prepare an incident report, recommend an initial severity level, or notify the team responsible for an affected system.

These activities may seem ordinary, but they can take a large amount of an analyst's time. An investigation may require checking several dashboards, comparing login records, reviewing device activity, and searching for threat information. An agent that gathers these details into one clear report can help a human analyst understand the situation sooner and spend more time on the evidence that matters.

Some defensive actions can also be automated when the evidence is strong and the action has been approved in advance. For example, an agent could temporarily block a confirmed malicious domain or isolate a standard workstation when multiple reliable signals show an active compromise. These actions can limit an attacker's opportunity to cause damage. The agent should still use restricted permissions, record its actions, and follow a tested process for recovery.

Not every response should receive the same level of autonomy. Collecting logs is generally less risky than shutting down a production database, disabling an administrator account, or isolating an entire network segment. The greater the potential damage, the more confidence and oversight the decision should require. A strong design separates permission to investigate from permission to make disruptive changes.

I would therefore introduce autonomy in stages. At first, the agent could analyze events and suggest actions without changing systems. After testing shows that its recommendations are reliable, it could be allowed to perform narrow, reversible actions in defined situations. Broader permissions should come only after careful review. This allows an organization to gain speed without treating every AI decision as automatically trustworthy.

Why Human Judgment Still Matters

Cybersecurity is not only about identifying malicious activity. Analysts also need to understand what an organization does, which services are essential, and what might happen if a system becomes unavailable. A response may stop an attacker but also interrupt payroll, medical services, customer transactions, or other important work. These consequences cannot always be judged from a security alert alone.

Consider a server that supports a critical business application. If there are signs that it has been compromised, isolating it may contain the threat. However, the same action could interrupt a service used by hundreds of people. A human analyst may need to examine the evidence, contact the system owner, consider safer alternatives, and coordinate the response with other teams. These decisions require both technical knowledge and an understanding of the organization's priorities.

Human judgment is especially important when an incident is unfamiliar. A possible zero-day attack, a suspected insider threat, or conflicting security signals may not fit an existing response procedure. Analysts must consider alternative explanations, identify what remains unknown, and decide whether the evidence is strong enough to justify a particular action. They must also be ready to change course when new evidence appears.

Another danger is automation bias: people may trust a machine's recommendation because it sounds confident or appears more informed than they are. Human oversight is not meaningful if an analyst simply approves every action without checking the evidence. The person supervising the agent needs enough training, time, and authority to question a recommendation, stop an unsafe process, and explain why a different response is necessary.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework discusses trustworthiness qualities such as validity and reliability, safety, security, accountability, transparency, and explainability (Tabassi, 2023). In a SOC, these ideas mean analysts should be able to review why an agent recommended or performed an action, which evidence it used, what assumptions it made, and where uncertainty remains.

Human responsibility also includes privacy and legal obligations. An agent may encounter sensitive information during an investigation, but that does not mean every person on the security team should have unrestricted access to it. Analysts must preserve relevant evidence, respect access rules, follow organizational policies, and make sure incident handling remains accountable.

To me, a good SOC pilot is neither a person who approves every AI recommendation nor someone who refuses to use automation. It is someone who knows when the agent can safely handle a task, when more evidence is needed, and when the possible consequences are too serious to leave to an uncertain automated decision.

The Problem With Incorrect Asset Information

One of the most important ideas in the IBM article is that an agent needs context before deciding how to respond to an alert. Knowing that a device is behaving suspiciously is only part of the problem. The agent also needs to know what the device does, how important it is, who owns it, which applications depend on it, and whether disruption would affect essential services.

A standard employee workstation and a server that supports a critical application may show similar warning signs, but the consequences of isolating them can be very different. If the agent does not know the device's role, it may select a response that makes sense from the alert alone but is wrong for the organization. The quality of an automated decision is therefore tied to the quality of its context.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 highlights the importance of identifying and managing assets, including hardware, software, services, and systems, and understanding their significance to cybersecurity risk management (Pascoe et al., 2024). This guidance matters for autonomous SOCs because an agent cannot make an informed decision if the organization does not maintain a reliable picture of the environment it is supposed to protect.

Imagine that an inventory incorrectly labels a critical application server as an ordinary workstation. During an incident, an agent might trust that record and isolate the server automatically. Although the action could stop suspicious activity, it might also interrupt an essential service. The agent may have followed its instructions correctly, but the outcome would still be harmful because the information behind its decision was wrong.

The opposite error is also serious. Suppose an ordinary workstation is incorrectly marked as highly critical. The agent might avoid isolating it because it assumes the action would cause a major outage. An attacker could then continue operating from the compromised device. In both cases, incorrect data can push the agent toward a harmful decision: acting too aggressively or waiting too long.

Asset records can become inaccurate for many reasons. A device may be renamed, moved to another department, or repurposed without updating the inventory. Ownership information may be missing, application dependencies may not be documented, or two security tools may disagree about the same device. Even an accurate record can become unreliable if it is old and no one has checked whether it still reflects the current environment.

Missing information should not give the agent permission to guess. Instead, it should mark unknown details as unknown, check trusted systems for confirmation, compare sources, and report when records conflict or have not been updated recently. It should separate facts from assumptions and explain which missing details prevent it from making a safe decision. Confidence in an AI-generated answer is not a replacement for evidence.

If a high-impact action depends on information that cannot be verified, the agent should pause that action and escalate the incident to a human analyst. Depending on the situation, it might still take a preapproved, low-impact step to reduce risk while waiting for review. The exact fallback should be decided in advance; it should not improvise a potentially disruptive action simply because its workflow expects an answer.

Organizations must therefore treat asset inventories as living records, not documents that are completed once and forgotten. They should assign clear ownership, update records when systems change, reconcile data across tools, document service dependencies, and regularly check whether critical information remains accurate. Reliable asset context is part of the security foundation. Without it, even a powerful AI agent can automate the wrong decision faster.

Making Automation Responsible

A responsible SOC should be evaluated by more than the number of alerts it closes or the speed at which it reacts. Organizations should measure whether actions are correct, how often they create unnecessary disruption, whether evidence is preserved, how frequently agents need human correction, and whether the incident was ultimately contained. Fast responses are useful only when they are appropriate.

Before an agent is allowed to act independently, it should be tested against realistic incidents, missing or incorrect asset records, conflicting evidence, and unusual conditions. Testing should include situations where the safest action is to do nothing automatically and ask for help. An agent that performs well on routine examples may still fail when the environment changes or when information is deliberately misleading.

Organizations should define action limits, require approval for high-impact decisions, use least-privilege permissions, keep clear audit logs, and maintain recovery procedures. Analysts must be able to pause an agent, review its activity, and reverse an action when possible. Teams should also examine failures and near misses so that playbooks, asset records, and controls can be improved.

The system needs a reliable escalation path. If essential context is missing, a security tool is unavailable, or the agent cannot determine whether an action is safe, it should stop short of that high-impact action and request review. This "fail safely" approach is not a weakness; it prevents the system from treating uncertainty as a reason to take a risk.

These controls are consistent with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework's emphasis on identifying, measuring, and managing risk throughout an AI system's lifecycle (Tabassi, 2023). Responsible autonomy requires governance and monitoring after deployment, not just a successful demonstration before launch. Permissions should be reviewed as threats, systems, and business needs change.

Skills I Would Need to Become a SOC Pilot

To become a SOC pilot rather than be replaced by one, I would need more than the ability to operate AI tools. I would need a strong foundation in computer networks, operating systems, identity and access management, endpoint security, cloud environments, log analysis, and incident response. These skills would help me understand what an agent is investigating and check whether its conclusions match the evidence.

I would also need critical thinking and AI literacy. This means recognizing uncertainty, checking the quality and freshness of data, understanding the limits of automated systems, and challenging a recommendation when its supporting evidence is weak. Scripting and security automation would help me understand response playbooks, test them safely, and identify the possible consequences of proposed actions. Knowledge of asset management would be important too, because the agent's decisions depend on accurate information about devices and services.

Technical knowledge alone would not be enough. A SOC pilot must communicate clearly with system owners, document decisions, coordinate with other teams, and explain risks in language that people can understand. The role also requires patience and continuous learning because attackers, tools, and organizational environments keep changing. Just as importantly, an analyst must be willing to accept responsibility, admit uncertainty, and ask for help when the evidence is incomplete.

Personally, I want to become the kind of analyst who knows when to rely on automation and when to question it. AI may become very good at gathering evidence and carrying out routine procedures, but someone still needs to understand what that evidence means in the real world. My goal would be to develop the technical confidence, judgment, communication, and accountability needed to guide automation responsibly rather than compete with it at repetitive tasks.

Conclusion

The SOC pilot model is a realistic direction for cybersecurity when it is introduced carefully, with clear limits and meaningful human oversight. AI can help investigate routine alerts, gather evidence, organize information, and perform defined low-risk actions. However, unfamiliar attacks, conflicting evidence, critical systems, and decisions with serious consequences still require human judgment. The airline comparison is a helpful vision of supervised automation, but cybersecurity's changing and adversarial environment makes unrestricted autonomy risky.

The asset-context problem shows why. If an agent relies on incomplete or incorrect information, it may make a decision that appears logical but causes an outage or allows an attack to continue. Accurate inventories, tested response procedures, restricted permissions, audit trails, and clear escalation paths are therefore essential. I believe the future SOC should combine machine efficiency with human responsibility: let AI handle suitable routine work, but keep trained people ready to question, guide, and take control when the situation demands it. Becoming a SOC pilot means learning how to use automation without surrendering judgment to it.
