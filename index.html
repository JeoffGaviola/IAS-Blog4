Can We Trust the SOC Pilot? Evaluating Autonomous AI in Cybersecurity

October 14, 2026 · Critical Reflection · Agentic AI

AI can help security teams work faster, but responsible automation still depends on reliable information, clear limits, and people who know when to take control.

From Alert Handler to SOC Pilot

Imagine arriving at work and finding hundreds of cybersecurity alerts waiting to be investigated. Some may indicate a genuine attack, while others might be harmless activity or false alarms. A security analyst has to sort through all of them, find useful evidence, and decide which situations deserve immediate attention. When alerts continue arriving throughout the day, it becomes difficult to give every incident the attention it deserves.

This is the problem that caught my attention when I read Velisaris's (2025) IBM article about building an autonomous security operations center, or SOC. He compares the future of security operations to a modern airliner. Machines handle much of the routine work, while human analysts become "SOC pilots" who supervise operations and step in when something unfamiliar or complicated happens.

I find this comparison interesting because it does not necessarily mean that humans become unnecessary. Instead, their responsibilities change. Rather than spending most of their time repeating the same investigative steps, analysts can concentrate on unusual threats, important decisions, and situations in which an automated response might cause harm. In theory, this could help security teams respond more quickly without overwhelming their people.

However, I do not think the SOC pilot model should be treated as a promise that cybersecurity can eventually run without meaningful human involvement. AI agents can make mistakes, misunderstand information, and choose actions that are technically reasonable but operationally harmful. My position is that the model is realistic if organizations introduce it carefully, test it properly, and maintain clear boundaries around what an agent is allowed to do.

Where AI Can Act Independently

AI is most useful when the task is repetitive, the instructions are clear, and the possible consequences are limited. For example, an agent can collect relevant security logs, group duplicate alerts, summarize suspicious activity, compare events with known indicators of compromise, and gather details from endpoint or authentication tools. It can also prepare an incident report, assign an initial priority, or notify the team responsible for an affected system.

These tasks might not sound especially impressive, but they take up a considerable amount of an analyst's time. An investigation may require checking several dashboards, comparing login records, looking at device activity, and searching for information about a particular threat. An AI agent that can collect and organize this evidence helps analysts get a clearer picture of the situation sooner.

I believe this is one of the safest starting points for autonomous security operations. The agent is helping gather information rather than making an irreversible decision. Its output can still be checked, and a mistake in a preliminary summary is less likely to cause a major outage than a mistake in an automated shutdown.

Some response actions can also be automated when the evidence is strong and the action has been carefully approved in advance. For instance, a security system might temporarily block a confirmed malicious domain or isolate an ordinary workstation when several independent signals indicate an active compromise. This can reduce the time an attacker has to cause damage.

Still, an agent should not receive unrestricted administrative access simply because it can perform these tasks. Its permissions should be limited to what it needs, its actions should be recorded, and the organization should have a way to review what happened. Where possible, a response should also have a tested recovery procedure.

Velisaris (2025) describes an approach in which agents can investigate alerts, gather missing information, and use context to determine a suitable next step. That is more flexible than automating the same sequence of commands for every incident. However, the ability to investigate a threat is not the same as having the authority to make every decision about it. An agent collecting evidence and an agent shutting down a production database should not have the same level of freedom.

For that reason, I would introduce autonomy in stages. Gathering evidence and preparing reports can happen automatically. Limited defensive actions may be automated after thorough testing. Actions that could disrupt essential services, delete information, or affect many users should require stronger evidence and, when appropriate, human approval. The aim should be to automate the routine without allowing speed to become an excuse for careless decisions.

Why Human Judgment Still Matters

Cybersecurity is not only about finding malicious activity. It also involves understanding what an organization does, which services are important, and what could happen if a system becomes unavailable. A response that stops an attacker might also interrupt an important business process. That is where human judgment becomes essential.

Consider a server that supports payroll or a critical customer service. If the server shows signs of compromise, isolating it might contain the incident. However, doing so could also interrupt work for hundreds of people. A human analyst can examine the evidence, contact the system owner, evaluate alternative responses, and coordinate the decision with the people responsible for the service.

Human involvement is especially important when an incident is unfamiliar. A possible zero-day attack, a suspected insider threat, or several conflicting security signals may not fit an existing response procedure. Analysts need to consider alternative explanations, identify what remains unknown, and decide whether the available evidence is strong enough to support a particular action.

There is also a risk of relying too heavily on a system that sounds confident. If an agent recommends isolating a device and provides a convincing explanation, an inexperienced analyst might accept the recommendation without checking the underlying evidence. This is why human oversight must mean more than having someone watch a dashboard. The person responsible needs enough knowledge, time, and authority to question the system's conclusion.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology's AI Risk Management Framework identifies characteristics of trustworthy AI, including validity and reliability, safety, security, accountability, transparency, and explainability (Tabassi, 2023). Applied to a SOC, these principles support a basic expectation: security analysts should be able to understand why an agent recommended or performed an action, what information it used, and what limitations remain.

Human judgment is also needed to consider legal, privacy, and organizational responsibilities. A security agent might find personal information while investigating an incident, but that does not mean every person in the organization should have access to it. Analysts must know how to preserve relevant evidence while respecting access restrictions and applicable policies.

To me, the best SOC pilot is not someone who approves every AI recommendation or someone who refuses to use automation. It is a person who understands both the strengths and weaknesses of the system. The analyst should know when to let the agent handle a routine task, when to request further evidence, and when to take control because the potential consequences are too serious to leave to an uncertain automated decision.

The Problem With Incorrect Asset Information

One of the most important ideas in the IBM article is that an agent needs context before deciding how to respond to an alert. Knowing that a device is behaving suspiciously is only part of the problem. The agent also needs to know what the device does, how important it is, who is responsible for it, and which services depend on it.

A regular employee workstation and a server supporting a critical application may show similar warning signs, but the consequences of isolating them can be very different. Without reliable asset information, an agent may choose a response that seems reasonable based on the alert but is wrong for the organization.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 emphasizes identifying and managing assets, including hardware, software, services, and systems. It also supports understanding the importance of those assets when organizations prioritize cybersecurity risks (Pascoe et al., 2024). This is directly relevant to autonomous SOC operations because an agent cannot make an informed response decision if the organization does not maintain a reliable picture of the environment it is supposed to protect.

Imagine that a company's asset inventory incorrectly labels a critical application server as an ordinary workstation. During an incident, an AI agent may trust this record and isolate the server automatically. The immediate action might stop suspicious activity, but it could also interrupt an essential service. The agent would have followed the information it was given, yet the result could still be harmful.

The reverse mistake is just as concerning. Suppose an ordinary workstation is incorrectly marked as highly critical. The agent might hesitate to isolate it because it believes that doing so would create a major outage. Meanwhile, an attacker may continue operating from the compromised device. In this case, incorrect context could delay an appropriate response.

Asset information can become inaccurate for many reasons. A device might have been renamed, moved to another department, or repurposed without updating the inventory. Ownership details may be missing, dependencies may not have been documented, or different security tools may contain conflicting records. The information might also be old enough that it no longer represents the current environment.

Incomplete information should never be treated as permission for the AI agent to guess. The agent should identify missing details, compare records from trusted systems, check when the information was last verified, and explain where sources disagree. It should distinguish confirmed facts from assumptions instead of presenting an uncertain classification as if it were unquestionably correct.

If the information needed to support a high-impact action cannot be verified, the agent should request human review or follow a predefined, low-impact containment procedure. It should not perform an irreversible action simply because its automated workflow expects a decision.

Organizations therefore need to treat asset inventories as living records rather than documents that are completed once and forgotten. They should assign responsibility for updating them, reconcile information across systems, review important changes, and investigate discrepancies. An agent is only as well informed as the information available to it, so reliable context is a basic security requirement, not an optional extra.

Making Automation Responsible

A responsible autonomous SOC should be evaluated by more than the number of alerts it closes or the speed at which it reacts. Organizations should also look at whether the actions are correct, how often they cause unnecessary disruption, whether evidence is preserved, and how well analysts can review the agent's decisions.

Before giving an agent permission to act independently, an organization should test it against realistic incidents, incomplete asset records, conflicting evidence, and unexpected situations. It should also establish clear thresholds for actions that require human approval. Every important action should produce an audit record explaining what triggered it, what information supported it, and what happened afterward.

The system should also have a clear way to stop unsafe operations. If essential context is missing, a required security tool is unavailable, or the agent cannot determine whether an action is safe, it should escalate the situation rather than continue blindly. Human analysts should be able to pause automated actions and investigate what has gone wrong.

These safeguards do not defeat the purpose of automation. They help establish where autonomy is justified. An organization can gradually give an agent more responsibility when testing and experience show that it handles a particular type of task reliably. If its performance changes or its environment becomes different, those permissions should be reviewed.

This approach is consistent with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which encourages organizations to identify, evaluate, and manage AI risks throughout the system's lifecycle (Tabassi, 2023). Responsible autonomy is not just about building a capable agent. It also requires governance, testing, monitoring, and accountability after the agent has been deployed.

Skills I Would Need to Become a SOC Pilot

To become a SOC pilot rather than be replaced by one, I would need more than basic knowledge of AI tools. I would first need a strong foundation in cybersecurity, including computer networks, operating systems, identity and access management, endpoint security, cloud environments, log analysis, and incident response. These skills would help me understand what an agent is investigating and check whether its conclusions match the available evidence.

I would also need to develop critical thinking and AI literacy. That means recognizing uncertainty, checking the quality and freshness of information, understanding how automated responses can fail, and being willing to challenge an AI recommendation when the evidence does not support it. Scripting and security automation would also be valuable because I would need to understand response playbooks, test them safely, and recognize the possible consequences of a proposed action.

Technical knowledge alone would not be enough. A SOC pilot must communicate clearly with system owners, document decisions, coordinate with other teams, and explain risks in a way that helps people make informed choices. The role also requires patience and continuous learning because attackers, defensive technologies, and organizational environments keep changing.

Personally, I would want to become the kind of analyst who knows when to rely on automation and when to question it. AI may become very good at collecting evidence and carrying out routine procedures, but someone still needs to understand what that evidence means in the real world. My goal would be to develop the judgment and technical confidence needed to supervise AI responsibly rather than compete with it at repetitive tasks.

Conclusion

The SOC pilot model is a realistic direction for cybersecurity, provided it is introduced with clear limits and meaningful human oversight. AI can help with repetitive investigations, gathering evidence, organizing alerts, and performing carefully defined low-risk actions. However, unfamiliar attacks, conflicting information, critical services, and high-impact decisions still require human judgment.

The asset-context problem is especially important. If an agent relies on incomplete or incorrect information, it may make a decision that appears logical but creates an outage or allows an attack to continue. Accurate inventories, reliable evidence, careful testing, and clear escalation procedures are therefore essential to responsible automation.

I see the future SOC as a partnership between machine efficiency and human judgment. Machines can handle much of the routine work, while analysts focus on understanding unusual situations, weighing consequences, and making responsible decisions. Becoming a SOC pilot means building the technical knowledge, critical thinking, communication, and accountability needed to guide automation rather than simply trust it.
